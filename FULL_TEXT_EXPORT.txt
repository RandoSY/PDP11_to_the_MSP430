Publication and technical notice
Final editorial textbook edition, October 2026. Prepared for independent study and laboratory instruction. No
ISBN or external publisher is asserted. The PDP-11 and DEC operating systems are discussed historically; DEC,
TI, Raspberry Pi, and their products are identified solely to describe the technology.
Examples labeled CANDIDATE are educational firmware and require target compilation and on-bench electrical
verification before they are used. The included Python examples and host-side controller logic may be tested
without the physical board, but host testing does not establish timing or electrical behavior on the MSP430.
This course uses 3.3 V logic, a safe LED output, and low-voltage signals. It does not qualify any design for
industrial safety or mains-voltage actuation. Inspect the physical board, power source, and signal mappings prior
to any connection.
How to use this textbook
Each chapter begins with a guiding question and essential vocabulary, develops the theory using physical or
historical examples, offers a source listing where appropriate, and ends with a field laboratory. The lab has a
prediction stage, a bounded procedure, an evidence gate, and a reproducible worksheet. Instructor keys are
collected in the back of the book rather than interleaved with student work.
The laboratory sequence is deliberately staged. A successful 1 kHz frequency measurement does not validate
1-Wire microsecond timing. A correct CRC does not prove temperature accuracy. A working software state
machine is not a certified industrial controller.
Learning pathways and prerequisites
The course is designed for an instructor-led introductory embedded-systems class, a self-directed electronics
bench, or an advanced historical-computing seminar. The same evidence standard applies to all three pathways,
but the order of presentation may change.
Architecture pathway
Read Chapters 1–4 first, then simulate the PDP-11 loop and interpret actual MSP430 assembly encodings before
wiring a board.
Physical-computing pathway
Begin with the safely verified 1 kHz apparatus in Chapter 5. Return to the historical architecture chapters as the
significance of addressing and interrupts becomes evident.
Instructor/operator pathway
Use Chapter 13, the laboratory evidence sheets, and Appendix F before the first class. Assign the physical
experiment only after the operator readiness gate has passed.

Preface — The instrument, the controller, and
the historical question
In 1970, Digital Equipment Corporation introduced the PDP-11 to a world in which minicomputers were
becoming practical instruments of science, manufacturing, and process control. Not every application demanded a
mainframe. A smaller computer, equipped with a useful instruction set, memory, a modular I/O system, and
software that responded to external events, could measure a physical process and participate in its control. That
possibility reshaped laboratories and industrial engineering.
More than half a century later, a small MSP430G2553 microcontroller can reproduce many of the conceptual
operations that made the PDP-11 attractive: read and write memory-mapped registers, respond to interrupts,
perform arithmetic on sensor values, and act upon an event at a pin. The MSP430 is not a miniature PDP-11. The
processors differ profoundly in bus architecture, resources, privilege models, peripheral arrangements, and
operating environment. But they belong in a productive conversation about what a general-purpose instruction set
can accomplish in the physical world.
This book places three machines in deliberate roles. The PDP-11 is the historical and architectural example,
explored through documents and optional simulation. The MSP430G2553 is the physical target, with instructions,
clocks, pins, and peripherals to understand. The RP2040/Pico is an observer and test instrument, not a substitute
for the controlled device. The PC, using Python and familiar development tools, analyzes observations and
produces engineering records.
The course adopts CAP: Capture raw observations with setup conditions; Analyze the calculations, limits, errors,
and uncertainty; Publish enough information for somebody else to reproduce the work. The pedagogical sequence
repeatedly begins with a prediction, then reveals a few relevant instructions, and only then moves to data.
The book is written for teachers, advanced learners, and practitioners who want to understand computers close to
the hardware. It is also suitable for a self-directed bench course. A student does not need to know a PDP-11 before
starting. A teacher does need the discipline to distinguish an example from an experiment and a measurement from
an assertion.
Conventions and evidence labels
Historical example means a representative concept or original source, not a claim that the identical code ran on a
particular industrial installation. Candidate firmware means plausible target code requiring a specific compiler,
linker setup, board pin map, and bench verification. Host-tested means the included Python algorithm unit tests
were executed without microcontroller hardware. Verified on bench may be claimed only when the teacher or
learner has preserved the actual programming log, wiring record, instrument configuration, and measurements.
Code listings use their own syntax; do not paste a PDP-11 listing into an MSP430 assembler. The user’s board pin
assignments are treated as the proposed custom-board netlist, not universal MSP430 pin definitions. Unless stated
otherwise, arithmetic exercises are examples, not actual readings.

Introduction — An engineering history that can
be measured
The theme of this book is that good computing architecture is best understood by following a real signal. A student
might admire the clean encoding of a PDP-11 instruction, but the deeper achievement was joining hardware,
programming tools, and industrial peripherals into one serviceable system. The challenge of an industrial computer
was never simply to calculate a numerical answer. It was to calculate the right result from valid inputs, within the
time allowed by the physical process, and to communicate or act on that result through an interface that could fail.
The machine in its environment
A steel rolling mill illustrates the distinction. Sensors can watch temperature and line speed while identification
data associates a reading with a particular piece of material. Actuating hardware can alter a setpoint, operate an
alarm, or request intervention. A supervisory computer helps organize these activities, but it is not the sensor, the
motor drive, the emergency stop, or the instrument by which its own behavior is tested. This separation was
important during the minicomputer era and remains important when a microcontroller costs very little.
For this book, the historical computer is the PDP-11; the learning processor is the MSP430G2553; and the
independent measuring device is a Raspberry Pi Pico. That is not a claim that three machines are interchangeable.
It is a practical division of jobs. The PDP-11 provides a rich history of memory-mapped hardware and
event-driven software. The MSP430 exposes comparable concepts through a small instruction set and on-chip
timers. The Pico makes timing and edges observable. A host PC offers programming, notebooks, tests, and
formatted publication.

Five questions for every experiment
1.
What physical quantity or event matters? Start with the phenomenon: a pulse, a switch closure, or a
changing temperature. What does the computer represent? Identify the bit pattern, register, memory
address, and timing reference. What action will the program request? Draw the causality from instruction
to peripheral to voltage. What can the independent observer establish? Distinguish a waveform's
frequency from pulse-width accuracy, and a valid CRC from sensor accuracy. How will the result be
reproduced? Save versions, pin maps, clock assumptions, raw observations, and a bounded conclusion.?
These questions produce a book that can be used on a yellow legal pad when hardware is absent. Every lab permits
a manual prediction and an order-of-magnitude estimate before the electrical result is available. Actual
measurements remain irreplaceable for claims about actual hardware.
How the course is arranged
Part I establishes the PDP-11 industrial and architectural precedent. Part II follows a sequence of progressively
richer MSP430 experiments: a timer signal; GPIO and switches; asynchronous event handling; UART telemetry;
1-Wire electrical signaling; sensor decoding; and control with hysteresis and fault policy. Part III gives instructors
a reproducible sequence and evaluation framework. Appendices provide register references, safety constraints, test
evidence, and code inventory. Students may enter through the physical experiment and return to the history after
seeing the need for addressing modes and interrupts.

1.1  The industrial problem before the processor
Consider a tank whose contents must be held between two temperature limits. A human operator could
periodically read a thermometer, note a change, and adjust a valve. But the process might demand decisions faster
than an operator can make them, or it might require hundreds of simultaneous observations, each recorded and
associated with a timestamp. Earlier solutions used electromechanical controllers, analog electronics, dedicated
hardware, and specialized process computers. The broader industrial challenge was to make measurement,
judgment, and action repeatable.
A programmable minicomputer changed the economics of this problem. One computing system could perform
multiple algorithms in software, collect measurements, produce logs, supervise alarms, and be reconfigured
without rewiring every decision. That did not abolish sensors, signal conditioning, relays, or safety interlocks.
Rather, it made the control logic programmable within a larger engineered system. A computer earns its place in
that system only when its failure modes and deadlines are understood.
DEC's PDP-11 family was a particularly influential example. It offered 16-bit computing, a useful and
comparatively orthogonal instruction set, modular interface options, and a growing ecosystem of programmers,
peripheral equipment, and operating systems. Later models differed from the original PDP-11/20; this was a
family, not a single fixed machine. A unit installed for laboratory acquisition might have very different memory,
interfaces, and operating software from one used in a manufacturing plant.
1.2  A canonical industrial control chain
Physical process → transducer → signal conditioning → interface → processor → output interface →
actuator → physical process. Every arrow represents an engineering contract. A transducer converts temperature,
pressure, force, or position to a measurable electrical quantity. The input interface gives the computer sampled
data or event signals. Software evaluates the data using a policy. The output interface can command a valve or
motor, subject to interlocks that should not rely solely on a software decision.
A separate observer may record time, raw samples, actuator state, fault flags, and energy consumption. This
distinction between control and observation will matter when we put a Pico beside the MSP430. An instrument
that reports 1,000 Hz has not demonstrated that the controlled process is safe; a controller that lights an LED has
not established the fidelity of a sensor.

1.3  UNIBUS, interrupts, and direct memory access
PDP-11 systems used modular interconnects including UNIBUS and, in other family members, Q-bus. The
original UNIBUS was more than a convenient connector. It organized requests for bus ownership, data movement,
peripheral registers, and interrupts. A disk or instrumentation interface could gain bus access; an interrupt could
signal that an external condition needed service; direct-memory transfers reduced repeated CPU involvement for
eligible peripherals. DEC's PDP-11 Bus Handbook documents bus arbitration and prioritized service in technical
detail.
One powerful architectural idea was memory-mapped I/O. A peripheral control register could be accessed with a
load or store instruction rather than a completely separate I/O instruction set. A programmer who understood
addressing and the meaning of each device register could operate a hardware interface. The processor's
instructions became the vocabulary for changing external machinery. In today's MSP430, register names such as
P1DIR, TA0CCR0, and UCA0TXBUF serve an analogous programming role, though there is no external UNIBUS and
the actual peripherals are different.
Another idea was priority. Not every event could wait for a periodic software poll. A machine could interrupt
ongoing work to service an event; a real-time operating system could schedule a task with greater urgency than
routine reporting. This did not guarantee deadlines by itself. Priority rules, worst-case service time, shared data,
and I/O latency still determined whether the control system met its obligations.

1.4  Industrial history without mythology
The PDP-11's industrial importance can be described precisely without calling it a universal solution. It appeared
in laboratories, test stands, process monitoring, manufacturing, and machine control. DEC manuals for real-time
systems explicitly discussed applications such as refinery and steel-processing control. But a successful installed
system was never just a CPU and an operating-system disk. It included electrical isolation, transducers, operator
interfaces, maintenance procedures, field wiring, software revisions, and emergency fallbacks.
This is the standard we should import into a teaching lab. We do not demonstrate industrial reliability by making a
thermostat LED blink. We demonstrate a first principle: deterministic local decisions, explicit sensor validity,
controlled output states, observed timing, and evidence of what happens on failure. Safety-critical industrial
control requires far more engineering and certification than this book attempts.

Case study: an imagined rolling-mill inspection station
Suppose a continuously moving steel strip passes a thermal inspection station. A pyrometer produces a
temperature signal, a length encoder tracks the strip position, and an alarm must be associated with the correct
section of material. A computer can help associate a particular sample with a position, apply the inspection rule,
and make a record. That does not imply the computer itself should directly energize heavy machinery. A
physically independent protective circuit is a separate engineering obligation.
For the course, reduce the plant to a harmless model. A learner presses a switch to represent the arriving strip; a
timer counts intervals; a temperature reading is captured; an LED signifies that a limit has been exceeded. The
value of the exercise is preserving the full information chain, including time and uncertainty, while stripping away
hazardous actuation.
The important historical contribution of the PDP-11 was not a particular factory algorithm. It was an adaptable and
maintainable combination of CPU, I/O, storage, software, and application engineering. The computer could be
reprogrammed to solve a different instrumentation problem without redesigning the entire decision apparatus.
Industrial automation: why an entire computer system mattered
A PDP-11 installation could serve as a data-collection station, a supervisory controller, a laboratory processor, or
the host of a larger process-control system. These roles must not be conflated. In a data-acquisition station,
measurements may be recorded for later decisions. A supervisory controller can propose setpoints for local
controllers. In direct digital control, the computer participates more directly in choosing actuating commands.
Each arrangement requires a different response to a lost sample, a broken cable, or a processor restart.
The DEC architecture made these configurations practical because the machine was not limited to one fixed
peripheral arrangement. UNIBUS connected multiple devices in a consistent architectural framework;
bus-mastering interfaces could move data efficiently; vectored interrupts allowed peripheral events to direct
software attention. This is system architecture, not merely a fast instruction set. A laboratory temperature task does
not require a UNIBUS, but it benefits from the same habit of defining a processor-to-device contract.
Use the historical rolling-mill case as an engineering design exercise. First separate measurements (thermocouple
or pyrometer), identification (the strip section being measured), calculation (a limit and rate-of-change rule), and
action (alarm or process-control request). Then add an independent protective layer for dangerous outcomes.
Finally record which statements are historical capabilities and which are features of the classroom model. This
discipline prevents an attractive analog from becoming an invented historical reconstruction.
PDP-11 hardware and the industrial interface contract
A CPU instruction that reads a device register is only the last link of a long measurement chain. Analog inputs
require excitation or signal conditioning, conversion, appropriate reference voltages, grounding, and shielding.
Digital contacts require defined polarity, isolation where required, and a known response to an open circuit. An
external device may be slower than a CPU; software must account for settling, busy status, completion flags, and
lost interrupts.
A useful classroom specification identifies, for each input, its physical variable, electrical representation,
engineering units, normal range, valid-update interval, failure state, and timestamp origin. For each output it
records the requested action, actuator interface, safe state, and independent limits. The PDP-11 hardware tradition
was valuable because programmable logic and industrial interfaces could be integrated, not because software could
ignore the plant. The MSP430 student should adopt exactly this interface-contract habit at a far smaller scale.

2.1  Eight registers and a coherent model
The classic PDP-11 programmer sees eight 16-bit registers. R0–R5 are available for general computations; R6 is
the stack pointer (SP); R7 is the program counter (PC). The processor status word records condition information
such as negative, zero, overflow, and carry. The fact that the PC and SP appear as addressable registers is not an
incidental flourish. Combined with the addressing modes, it gives compact ways to express constants, relative
addresses, stacks, and subroutine behavior.
Think of a register as an immediately accessible storage location inside the processor, not a variable in RAM. A
MOV moves a value; ADD combines a source and a destination; condition codes describe the outcome. An instruction
can operate on an ordinary register or a memory value selected through an addressing mode. The memory
arrangement and peripheral mapping may vary by PDP-11 model and memory-management configuration; these
introductory exercises avoid assuming an entire system layout.
2.2  Addressing modes are the real lesson
PDP-11 addressing modes include register, register deferred, autoincrement, autoincrement deferred,
autodecrement, autodecrement deferred, indexed, and indexed deferred. On many double-operand instructions, the
source and destination can use the same broad palette. That symmetry is called orthogonality: many operations can
apply to operands selected in the same ways. Orthogonality is not absolute, and device or instruction constraints
remain, but the design is remarkably elegant.
A register operand such as R2 means the value currently held in the register. (R2) denotes the contents of memory
at the address in R2. (R2)+ accesses memory and then advances R2, typically by the operand size (with special
cases).
4(R2) adds an offset to the register to locate an operand. With PC-relative forms, the same principles can address code or data near
the instruction itself.
The PDP-11's user-visible design demonstrates how architecture can reduce a programmer's need to write separate
sequences for every kind of memory access. But supporting many addressing modes affects instruction encoding,
hardware, timing, and memory transactions. We will see an MSP430 with more registers but fewer flexible
destination modes.

2.3  Worked program: sum five integers
The short MACRO-11-style illustration in code/pdp11/01_register_loop.mac computes 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15
decimal. R0 counts down; R1 accumulates. The SOB instruction subtracts one from a register and branches back if
nonzero. Because the PDP-11 traditionally displays addresses and values in octal, 15 decimal is 17 octal. The
listing is a teaching example, not a verified simulator session.
Read the program twice. On the first pass, write every value R0 and R1 will hold. On the second pass, count how
many times ADD executes and explain why the branch eventually stops. Then identify the machine state at HALT.
Notice that no variable called sum is necessary for this exercise: the registers themselves are the state.
2.4  Condition codes and signed arithmetic
A processor can represent a 16-bit pattern as an unsigned quantity or a signed two's-complement quantity. The bit
pattern alone does not tell the programmer which interpretation is intended. Zero indicates a zero result; negative
generally tracks the most significant bit; overflow matters when signed arithmetic cannot be represented; carry
matters for unsigned arithmetic or multiword operations. It is a mistake to treat these status flags as
interchangeable.
Exercise. Add 1 to 0x7FFF in a 16-bit signed context. What bit pattern results? Why does the overflow flag matter
even if the resulting bit pattern can be read as a positive unsigned integer? Then subtract one from zero and discuss
borrow and negative flag behavior using the processor manual's exact definitions.
2.5  Simulation laboratory
An optional Open SIMH PDP-11 configuration permits exploration without a physical minicomputer. Install a
reputable emulator, follow its documentation to select a specific simulated CPU and console, and obtain a suitable
assembler/OS environment or use documented front-panel deposit/examine functionality. Do not assume that a
MACRO-11 source file can be pasted directly at a SIMH prompt: the simulator's command language is
distinct from an assembler, and assembling software may require an operating-system image or another tool.
The simplest learning artifact is a register trace. Write the instruction, the before-state, the expected after-state, and
the actual after-state. Avoid spending a full introductory week installing a historical operating system. An
instructor can preconfigure the machine, or substitute a pencil-and-paper execution trace when infrastructure is
unavailable.
CAP gate. Publish a register-by-register execution table and state whether the values came from a simulator, a
documented hand trace, or an actual machine.
Worked trace: five additions and a branch
A trace is more instructive than an unexplained correct total. Begin with R0 holding 5 and R1 holding 0. After the
first ADD, R1 becomes 5. SOB then decrements R0 to 4 and branches to the loop. The sequence of accumulator
values is 5, 9, 12, 14, 15. The sequence of loop-counter values after SOB is 4, 3, 2, 1, 0. On the final SOB, the zero
count suppresses the branch.
Why is the instruction sequence teachable? The loop counter resides in a register, arithmetic writes another
register, and the PC changes according to a branch condition. Nothing here is mystical. The useful skill is
accounting for each state transition. Trace the MSP430 counterpart with DEC R0 renamed to MSP430 R4; do not
assume the PC, SP, or assembler syntax are interchangeable.

Registers, stack, octal notation, and an emulator
Industrial or historical context
The PDP-11 had eight visible 16-bit registers. R6 acted as stack pointer, R7 as program counter, and R0 through
R5 were ordinary working registers. That compact and regular model remains one of the most teachable examples
of assembly language. The PDP-11 community's use of octal can initially distract learners whose later work is
mostly hexadecimal. Explain octal as a historical convention aligned to groups of three bits, not as a magical
hardware feature. A value of decimal 15 is octal 17, and the same 16-bit pattern can be written in several bases.
Model and technical reasoning
Trace the loop in code/pdp11/01_register_loop.mac before running it. Initially R0=5 and R1=0. The ADD
instruction adds the current count to the accumulator; SOB decrements the count and branches when the new result
is nonzero. Record each iteration and the instruction that changes R0. The expected final arithmetic total is
decimal 15. A halted simulator is evidence of instruction execution only when the program has actually been
assembled, loaded, and inspected.
Prediction before measurement
Write the values of R0 and R1 after every execution of SOB, before opening any simulator. State whether the last
iteration adds 1 or 0 and justify the order of operations.
Procedure and evidence gates
1.
Inspect the MACRO-11-style source file and underline register operands and immediate values.
2.
Use a PDP-11 simulator with documented CPU and loader configuration; select an appropriate assembler or a
verified binary input.
3.
Load the program and stop it at entry. Record PC, SP, R0 and R1.
4.
Step ADD and SOB separately for the first two iterations; note PC changes when the branch is taken.
5.
After HALT, inspect accumulator state, compare with paper arithmetic, and record the exact emulator command
sequence.
Interpretation questions
1.
How do the register aliases SP and PC affect ordinary arithmetic instructions?
2.

3.

4.

3.1  A process does not wait for a terminal
A commercial accounting program can often wait for an operator to type a command. A machine monitoring
molten steel or a pressure vessel has different obligations. A measurement may need to occur within a deadline; an
alarm may need to preempt a report; a sensor may fail at the worst possible instant. The distinction is not merely
whether a computer is fast, but whether its behavior is appropriately bounded and supervised.
The PDP-11 hosted several software environments. RT-11 served compact single-user, real-time and laboratory
uses. RSX-11M and RSX-11S are particularly instructive for industrial history because they exposed ideas of
tasks, scheduling, event-driven activity, and dedicated real-time environments. Other members of the family
supported interactive computing, business applications, and UNIX development. We should not teach those
environments as interchangeable or suggest every PDP-11 installation ran the same software.
3.2  Three kinds of time
Students often confuse clock frequency, execution time, and response latency. Clock frequency gives the rate of
timing events driving some processor or peripheral. Instruction execution time depends on the actual instruction,
selected operands, memory accesses, and implementation. Response latency is the time from an external event to
a meaningful system reaction. A fourth term, jitter, describes variations in event timing or latency. Industrial
systems need good answers for all four, not just an impressive processor clock specification.
Consider a system that samples pressure every 10 ms. A large average CPU capacity does not prove that every
sample arrives on time. A priority inversion, slow interrupt routine, or unbounded busy wait may cause a deadline
miss. A real-time operating system can help organize work but cannot remove physical constraints. A small
bare-metal MSP430 can also meet selected deadlines if its program is simple, measured, and carefully controlled.
3.3  Interrupts as promises with costs
An interrupt lets the CPU abandon its current instruction stream at a defined point, save enough state, and execute
service code. A reliable interrupt service routine (ISR) does not casually execute arbitrary work. It should usually
do the minimum needed to capture a state or acknowledge a device, then defer expensive formatting, calculations,
or communication until appropriate. The exact costs differ between PDP-11 models, MSP430 family members,
clock settings, and software conventions.
In RSX-11M, task priorities and event mechanisms help coordinate activities. In an MSP430 lab, a hardware timer
and ISR can provide a simpler demonstration of responsiveness. The lesson is not to recreate RSX-11M on a chip
with 512 bytes of SRAM. It is to identify which responsibilities should happen promptly and which can wait.

abstractions.

4.1  Architectural similarities that matter
The MSP430 has sixteen 16-bit register designators: R0/PC, R1/SP, R2/SR and special-purpose decoding,
R3/constant generator, and R4–R15 as twelve general working registers. The PDP-11 has eight
programmer-visible 16-bit registers, with PC and SP incorporated into the same general register numbering. Both
machines permit many source/destination operations and address memory through register-based modes. The
surface resemblance in assembly language is not an accident of spelling: the machines share powerful ideas about
registers and operands.
However, one must be precise. The MSP430 is not binary-compatible with the PDP-11 and cannot execute
PDP-11 machine code directly. Its source and destination addressing bits are asymmetric, its interrupt system and
peripherals differ, and its registers have specialized encodings. TI documentation historically noted that the
MSP430 can emulate much of the PDP-11 instruction functionality; that is not the same as running an unchanged
PDP-11 executable.

4.2  A side-by-side example
PDP-11: MOV #10,R4, ADD R4,R5, MOV (R5)+,R6. MSP430: MOV.W #10,R4, ADD.W R4,R5, MOV.W @R5+,R6. The
final line on both sides reads through a register-held pointer and advances that pointer. Syntax differs, and exact
encoded instructions differ, but the conceptual mapping is unusually close.
Why does the MSP430 have twelve general working registers? Its 16-bit instruction encoding assigns four bits to a
register number, allowing sixteen register choices. But those bits consume space. In the common two-operand
format, the source has two mode bits while the destination has just one, so the destination cannot use every source
addressing form. The PDP-11 uses three mode bits plus three register bits for either operand in many
double-operand instructions. That symmetry yields richer addressing while leaving fewer register numbers.

4.3  The constant generator is a design lesson

4.4  RISC and CISC: useful words, unhelpful labels

Why four more register-select bits change the design
An instruction format is a physical budget. A sixteen-bit instruction word cannot simultaneously allocate unlimited
opcode bits, register bits, mode bits, and size control. The PDP-11’s three-bit register identifier selects one of eight
registers; the MSP430’s four-bit field selects one of sixteen. Two named operands can therefore consume more of
the fixed instruction word on the MSP430.
The MSP430 preserves many elegant source forms while restricting destination addressing. R2 and R3 encodings
offer selected constants and special addressing operations. These are not inelegant afterthoughts to be memorized;
they are clues to the design pressure imposed by an economical instruction representation.
A learner should prove one encoding with the assembler listing instead of trusting this paragraph. Compile an
immediate constant, an indexed operation, and a register-register ADD. Record the instruction words and
extension words for each. Compare the source length with the stored machine-code length. That is a reproducible
way to discuss code density without asserting that one architecture is universally superior.
Instruction encoding laboratory: where did the bits go?
The PDP-11 used three bits for each register identifier and three bits for each operand addressing mode. Its regular
double-operand format made both operand fields six bits. In the MSP430 Format I representation, a four-bit source
register field and two source-mode bits coexist with a four-bit destination register field, only one destination-mode
bit, an opcode field, and a byte/word bit. The teaching question is not whether the later CPU has more registers but
how the architectural design spends the limited space in its sixteen-bit instruction word.

Industrial or historical context
The first laboratory on the real MSP430 builds a deliberately simple 1 kHz output on P1.6. A nominal 1 MHz
SMCLK clocks Timer_A. Up mode counts from 0 to CCR0, inclusive. CCR0=499 means 500 timer ticks. When
output mode 4 toggles TA0.1 once for each timer period, a complete square-wave cycle requires two toggles: the
ideal frequency is 1,000,000/(2 × 500), or 1000 Hz. The hardware timer changes the pin while the CPU can remain
in an idle loop. This distinction is essential: a timer-driven waveform is not a bit-banged software loop.
Model and technical reasoning
The frequency meter is a second system with its own timebase. Even if the target is nominally set to 1 MHz, actual
drift and temperature can change the signal. The Pico counts rising edges during a measured gate. Capture both
pulse count and elapsed duration, not only a formatted hertz number. A one-second gate gives roughly one-hertz
count resolution before clock and gate errors are considered; apparent extra decimal digits do not establish
matching accuracy. The duty cycle may depend on compare timing and output mode. Measure that separately
when a pulse-width tool is available.

5.2  Derive the expected frequency
Start with a nominal 1,000,000-Hz SMCLK. Timer_A in up mode counts from zero through CCR0 inclusive, giving
CCR0+1 timer ticks. Select TA0CCR0 = 499; the timer period is 500 microseconds. If output mode 4 toggles channel TA0.1 once
per timer period at a compare point CCR1, a full square-wave period requires two toggles. Thus the nominal output frequency is
f_out = f_SMCLK / [2 × (CCR0+1)] = 1,000,000 / (2 × 500) = 1,000 Hz.
A common mistake is to conclude that 500 ticks at 1 MHz produces a full 2-kHz square-wave cycle. It produces
one edge every 500 microseconds in this configuration, not a full high-low-high cycle. A second common mistake
is to treat exactly 1 MHz as a measured DCO frequency. Factory constants nominally configure a clock, but actual
oscillator behavior depends on calibration conditions, supply, temperature, and time.

Worked computation: count period is not square-wave period
Assume SMCLK is nominally 1 MHz and Timer_A counts through the sequence 0 to 499. That is 500 clocks per
counting interval. If the selected output mode toggles the output once each interval, the time between toggles is
500 microseconds. The time between matching rising edges is two intervals, or 1,000 microseconds. The nominal
frequency is therefore 1 kHz.
Now change CCR0 to 999 while keeping the same timer source. Each interval becomes 1,000 clocks; two intervals
make an output cycle, and the expected output becomes 500 Hz. This controlled intervention is more informative
than repeating the same measurement. It tests whether the observed circuit responds in the predicted direction and
magnitude.
Never substitute nominal clock assumptions for evidence. If the Pico gate depends on its own uncharacterized
crystal, a frequency disagreement can arise in either instrument. The first experiment is about generating and
comparing a waveform. Calibrating either timebase is a different experiment and must be described separately.

6.1  What a register write really means
In C, a statement such as P1DIR |= BIT0 looks like an ordinary expression. The left side represents a hardware
register at a defined address. BIT0 is a mask selecting one bit. The operator |= means read the current value, set
that bit, and write the result back without intentionally changing the other bits. A write to a GPIO direction register
can change the electrical function of a pin: input versus output. A write to an output register can change the output
latch level.
The corresponding MSP430 assembly typically uses bit-oriented operations such as BIS.B and BIC.B with a mask
and a direct hardware-register address. The PDP-11 had its own memory-mapped peripheral registers; the
vocabulary of addressable device state carries across even when instruction encodings and buses do not. The
student's mental model should always include the target memory address and the physical device beyond it.
6.2  An active-low switch
The project guide maps a switch to P2.0, with a connection to ground when pressed. To read this reliably, make
the pin an input, enable its internal resistor, and select pull-up. The idle input should read 1 and the pressed input 0,
assuming wiring matches the proposed netlist. This inversion surprises beginners until they draw the circuit. P2IN
&
BIT0 is a masked hardware read, not a declaration that a button is 'true' or 'false' by itself.
The source code/msp430/02_gpio_switch.c reads the switch and commands P1.0. Check LED polarity in the
actual board; logic 1 may not mean 'visibly illuminated' in every circuit. A voltmeter at the pin can distinguish
program logic from an LED installed backward or a missing current-limiting component. Build each example
separately, and do not reuse P1.6 for an LED if the frequency-meter lesson currently occupies it.

6.3  Volatile and changing external state
A hardware input can change between two processor instructions without any assignment in C. That is why
device-register definitions carry volatile semantics and why an optimizing compiler must not blindly reuse a
previously loaded value as though it were an ordinary local variable. volatile does not solve atomicity, protect
access from interrupts, create a memory barrier for all possible hardware, or make an operation fast. It tells the
compiler a narrower but essential truth about observable accesses.

7.1  When an external event interrupts a calculation
Imagine a filling station. A sensor reports that a container has reached its target level. The controller must close a
valve before the contents overflow. The correctness of the action depends on two dimensions: the valve closes, and
the command arrives in time. A beautifully structured program that misses the deadline is not a correct real-time
program. This distinction was central to the operating environments developed for the PDP-11, particularly
RSX-11M and RSX-11S, and it remains central to the MSP430.
A novice program might repeatedly inspect a switch: while (1) if (switch_pressed()) close_valve();.
Polling can be appropriate when the interval between polls is bounded and the application tolerates it. But the loop
can be delayed by display formatting, serial transmission, or unexpectedly slow arithmetic. Interrupts provide
another mechanism: when an event occurs, dedicated hardware redirects execution to a service routine. Neither
mechanism is magically deterministic. We must account for interrupt masking, instruction completion, nested
interrupts, resource sharing, and the time consumed by the ISR.
A PDP-11 installation might combine interrupts, device controllers, and RSX task priorities. The MSP430 has no
RSX executive in this example; instead, its vector table, peripheral interrupt flags, status register, and a small main
loop accomplish the necessary coordination. We should teach the responsibility that survives across both systems
rather than pretending their implementations are the same.

What students should conclude
Latency = event to service start. Jitter = variability in timing from one event to the next. A hardware timer output can have low
jitter even while an interrupt-driven software output looks noisy. Therefore “it averages correctly” does not answer whether a
protocol slot or control deadline was met.

7.2  Three kinds of timing
Elapsed time is the physical interval measured between events. CPU time is the interval spent executing
instructions. Response latency is the time between an external event and the intended reaction. The first two are
not enough to establish the third. In addition, jitter is variation in observed timing from one instance to another. A
control event can be timely on average and still occasionally miss a deadline.
For the 1-MHz clock used in the introductory lab, a cycle is nominally 1 microsecond. But it is incorrect to
conclude that an arbitrary C statement takes 1 microsecond. Instructions differ in cycle counts, access modes,
compiler output, and effects on interrupts. Instead, use a timer-based stimulus and a second GPIO pin as an
observation point; measure the separation between events using a pulse-width-capable instrument. Keep the CPU
clock assumption separate from the observation instrument's clock uncertainty.

7.4  Design exercise: a deadline budget
Suppose a hypothetical process requires response within 5 milliseconds. Allocate 1.0 ms for sampling, 0.5 ms for
computation, 0.5 ms for output update, and 1.0 ms for worst-case waiting. The sum is 3.0 ms, leaving 2.0 ms of
illustrative margin. Each number must eventually be replaced with measured or justified maxima. If the process
has a relay that takes 20 ms to release, the software budget alone is irrelevant; actuator physics must be included.
CAP gate. Show a measured interval distribution with a defined start event and end event, and report the
maximum seen—not just the mean. State what would be required to establish a true worst-case bound.
Jitter: compare two ways to move the same pin
Create one signal with a hardware timer compare output and another with an interrupt service routine that toggles a
GPIO bit. Both may have similar average frequency. They are not equivalent instruments for precise edge
placement. The hardware output-compare edge is tied to timer hardware; the software edge is delayed by interrupt
recognition, entry, other interrupts, and the instructions preceding the port write.
Capture many edges with a timing-capable instrument. For each adjacent pair, compute the interval. Report the
minimum, maximum, and range as well as the mean. If the measurement resolution is coarse, say so. An attractive
average cannot conceal unstable edges.
This is also a practical bridge back to RSX-11M. The software problem of selecting a task and the electrical
problem of meeting a deadline are related, but not identical. Priority helps organize response, while measurement
demonstrates whether a particular built system meets its declared timing budget.
A concrete deadline budget
Consider an event at an MSP430 input. Before useful application code executes, the signal must reach the pin and
meet electrical requirements, the interrupt mechanism must recognize it, the CPU must complete or interrupt its
current activity as defined by the architecture, and context must be saved. The handler then performs instructions
and may wake lower-priority code. If a handler toggles a measurement pin only after inspecting a flag, that
inspection is part of the observed latency.
Write a budget using illustrative numbers: input recognition 5 microseconds, entry and context 8 microseconds,
handler work 20 microseconds, and exit or handoff 7 microseconds. The nominal sum is 40 microseconds. It is
only a paper model: real intervals must be obtained from the target clock, interrupt settings, compiler-generated
code, and a timing instrument. If another operation can postpone interrupts by 70 microseconds, the model must
include that delay in its worst-case bound.
Students should capture at least twenty time intervals rather than reporting a single convenient one. Record not
only an average but minimum, maximum, the number of samples, and the instrument time resolution. A measured
maximum over twenty events is evidence about those events; it is not a proof of the absolute worst case under all
legal operating conditions.

dominate a tight loop. That is why industrial control should avoid placing blocking report functions inside
time-critical interrupt routines.

9.1  The discipline of releasing a line
The Dallas/Maxim/Analog Devices 1-Wire protocol combines a physical electrical rule with a timed conversation.
The master and device may pull the data line low. Under normal externally powered operation, neither should
force the shared line high against the other; instead, the pull-up restores the high level after release. The distinction
matters because the microcontroller output latch and output-direction register can cause an electrical conflict if the
pin is inadvertently configured as a driven high output.
For this curriculum, use a genuine, externally powered DS18B20, with its VDD and ground correctly connected
and a verified data pull-up to 3.3 V. The existing board's P1.7 net is a plausible location because the prior netlist
assigns it an approximately 4.7-kΩ pull-up, but this must be confirmed on the actual board and checked for I²C
conflicts. The sensor package pinout must come from its manufacturer datasheet, not from a generic drawing.
9.2  The primitive operations
A master needs only a few physical primitives: reset, sense presence, write one bit, and read one bit. Byte
operations then build on bit operations, least-significant-bit first. The conceptual state transitions are:
CODE EXAMPLE  ·  in-text excerpt
IDLE high -> MASTER LOW -> MASTER RELEASE -> DEVICE RESPONSE -> IDLE high
The reset interval and the read/write slots each have bounds specified in the datasheet. The critical time is the
voltage transition observed at the pin. A C delay loop that says wait_us(6) is merely a hypothesis about the
signal. Compiler optimization, overhead, interrupts, clock frequency, and pin capacitance can change what
actually occurs. The physical test should show reset low time, release interval, presence response, a write-1 slot, a
write-0 slot, and the read sampling point.

pulse-width capture or logic-analyzer program. The measurement instrument's edge timing resolution, input

Why the 1-Wire signal is not ordinary serial data
A 1-Wire device and its master cooperate electrically on one pulled-up data conductor. A normal idle is high
because a resistor supplies current, not because every participant drives the wire high. To start a low pulse, the
master configures an output that sinks current. To release the line, the master stops driving it low, and the pull-up
returns it high.
This electrical convention matters to firmware: a line that remains actively driven high cannot safely coexist with a
device attempting to signal low. The driver must separate “drive low” from “release.” A reset/presence exchange is
consequently a time sequence of ownership changes, not simply a UART byte.
Measure the reset-low interval, the sensor response window, and representative write-one, write-zero, and read
slots at the physical pin. Do not infer microsecond validity from observing a successful high-level temperature
conversion. A 1 kHz frequency counter is useful elsewhere, but an edge-time capture or scope is necessary for the
1- Wire timing claim.
Engineering the one-wire port rather than copying a delay loop
Start with the electrical rule: a bus master must pull DQ low or release it. The master should never drive DQ
actively high when the sensor may also drive it low. For the proposed MSP430 board the candidate DQ net is P1.7;
inspect the physical pull-up and remove other active devices from that shared I/O line before applying voltage.
Keep sensor VDD connected to 3.3 V for the introductory project; parasite-power mode introduces different
power-delivery conditions.
The candidate 1-Wire transport uses Timer_A0 in continuous mode as a nominal microsecond counter. That
imposes a real resource conflict: it cannot simultaneously use the same timer configuration as the earlier Timer_A0
1 kHz exercise. The exercises are intentionally separate firmware images. An engineer may later choose another
timer or a dedicated PIO probe, but must document the revised ownership and routing.
For a write-one slot, a short low pulse is followed by release; for a write-zero slot, the low interval occupies most
of the slot. A read slot begins with a low pulse, a release, and a sample taken during the device's defined response
window. Show those phases as separate regions on a measured waveform. A logic analyzer used at an inadequate
sample rate can make a nominal 6-microsecond pulse appear to have different widths, so characterize the capture
instrument too. Bus capacitance, the resistor, wiring length, and the sensor's input characteristics all affect rise
time.

The Electrical Grammar of One Wire
Bus release, timing slots, and sensor presence
Industrial or historical context
The DS18B20 1-Wire protocol is exceptionally educational because the wire is not simply driven to zero or one by
one participant. The idle line is high because a pull-up supplies current. The master and devices assert low, then
release the bus. A master that actively drives high during a device response can create electrical contention. The
simplest correct mental model is therefore not “send one and zero”; it is “pull low and release”, followed by
carefully timed sampling. That is exactly the kind of low-level behavior the PDP-11 era taught engineers to
examine at hardware interfaces.
Model and technical reasoning
The device data sheet defines reset, presence, write-zero, write-one, and read time slots. A reset begins with a
master low interval of at least 480 µs. After release, a sensor may assert a presence pulse in a defined response
window. A valid software delay count alone is insufficient evidence: rising edges are influenced by the pull-up
resistor, bus capacitance, output-direction switching, and compiler-generated code. At the present 1 MHz DCO
stage, source-level cycle delays must be bench-characterized. Work with externally powered three-wire mode
rather than parasite power initially.

Sketch the reset and presence waveform with ownership labels. Mark the interval in which the master drives low,
the interval in which nobody actively drives, and the interval in which the sensor can pull low.

10.1  A conversion is more than two bytes
The DS18B20 stores its current result in a scratchpad. After a completed conversion, the two least-significant
scratchpad bytes contain a signed fixed-point value. At 12-bit resolution the raw unit is 1/16 °C; the remaining
scratchpad bytes include alarm/configuration values, reserved fields, and an 8-bit CRC. The CRC covers the first
eight scratchpad bytes and is supplied in byte nine. A successful CRC check detects many data-transfer faults, but
does not certify the sensor's thermometric accuracy or its thermal coupling to the environment.
The physical reading sequence for one confirmed DS18B20 is: reset/presence; Skip ROM 0xCC; Convert T 0x44;
wait for conversion; reset/presence; Skip ROM 0xCC; Read Scratchpad 0xBE; read nine bytes; verify CRC; decode
temperature. If multiple 1-Wire devices are attached, Skip ROM is not the correct general addressing strategy for a
read. Use ROM identification and device selection.

10.2  Decode signed fixed point without hiding arithmetic

10.3  CRC as a protocol laboratory
The code code/python/ds18b20_math.py provides a host algorithm for the reflected Dallas CRC-8 polynomial,

Decode a negative temperature without magic
The DS18B20’s two low scratchpad bytes form a signed sixteen-bit integer representing sixteenths of a degree
Celsius at 12-bit resolution. For example, the synthetic bytes 0x5E 0xFF make 0xFF5E. Interpreted as an unsigned
integer this is 65374; interpreted as signed two’s complement it is −162. Dividing by 16 gives −10.125 °C.
This is an ideal boundary between embedded C and Python. The MCU should validate the bytes and publish a
signed raw value with a validity flag. Python can present the temperature, graph a series, and perform further
calculations. Neither language makes a reading accurate simply because it prints more digits.
Separate four claims: transmission integrity (CRC), computation correctness (fixed-point arithmetic), conversion
completion (the device did finish taking a new reading), and measurement quality (the sensor accurately represents
the physical object under test). Each claim requires a different type of evidence.
Worked complete sensor transaction: commands, waiting, and validity
A transaction must represent the whole physical operation. After an idle-high check, issue a reset and look for a
genuine presence pulse. With exactly one known sensor on the bus, the Skip ROM command (0xCC) can be used,
followed by Convert T (0x44). The simplified candidate waits the maximum 750 ms for a 12-bit conversion with
the sensor externally powered. Reset again, select the one sensor, issue Read Scratchpad (0xBE), and collect all
nine bytes. Check the CRC of bytes zero through seven against byte eight and then decode the signed raw
temperature value.
This sequence deliberately makes the limitations visible. A successful CRC does not prove that the convert
command was actually completed, because old or power-up scratchpad data can be returned. A result of 85 °C can
be a legitimate physical measurement or the DS18B20's initial register representation; the software must establish
that a new conversion was commanded and allowed to finish. Missing presence, CRC failure, unexpected sensor
count, and stale data are separate fault categories that deserve distinct status codes and records.
The companion 10_ds18b20_transaction_CANDIDATE.c is deliberately blocking and limited to one externally
powered sensor. Its delay assumes an appropriate nominal 1 MHz MCU clock, and its transport functions are
imported from the unvalidated 1-Wire module. A real product would introduce a nonblocking schedule and a
sensor identity check. The teaching version offers an inspectable transaction before layering on an event-driven
application.

11.1  A controller needs memory
A naive thermostat uses one threshold: if temperature is above 29 °C, activate cooling, otherwise turn cooling off.
A noisy sensor close to 29 °C can cause repeated rapid on/off decisions, a problem called chatter. Hysteresis gives
the controller a memory of its previous state. In our educational example cooling becomes requested when
temperature rises to 30 °C and remains requested until it falls to 28 °C. Between those values, the previous state is
retained.

control, the commanded output must measurably affect the controlled process, and the sensor must observe that
effect. A simulated output LED is a useful intermediate step; it is not a substitute for actuation.

11.3  Scheduling a process supervisor
One feasible architecture uses a periodic timer flag to schedule sensor work, a bounded state machine to initiate
and complete conversions, and a low-priority output path to transmit telemetry. A 750-ms conversion at 12-bit
resolution should not be implemented as a single interrupt handler that busy-waits for 750 ms. The controller can
issue conversion, return to other work, and collect data after completion. However, the 1-Wire timing-critical slots
do require carefully bounded microsecond-scale operations.
CODE EXAMPLE  ·  in-text excerpt
SAMPLE_IDLE -> START_CONVERSION -> WAIT_CONVERSION
            -> READ_SCRATCHPAD -> VALIDATE_CRC
            -> UPDATE_CONTROLLER -> REPORT -> SAMPLE_IDLE
                 | any error -> FAULT -> REPORT
This is the same architectural separation that real-time industrial systems demand: acquisition, decision, actuation,
and reporting have different timing and failure requirements. On a PDP-11 with RSX-11M, the responsibilities
might be distributed among driver interrupt service, prioritized tasks, and data-logging processes. On the MSP430
they can be functions in a compact event-driven loop. Compare the responsibilities, not the software scale.

Test at least four independent properties. Sensor integrity: disconnect DQ or corrupt a CRC and verify FAULT.
Control correctness: sweep temperatures upward and downward across both thresholds, verifying transitions.
Timing: measure acquisition interval and any output transition delay. Independence: disconnect the PC display
during an active experiment; the local MCU logic should continue its documented behavior. If it does not, the
architecture has unintentionally moved control into the observation path.
CAP gate. Record a state-transition table including invalid data and recovery, wiring, raw values, state outputs,

A fail-safe policy is a separate specification
A threshold controller with hysteresis remembers a state. A cooler is commanded ON at or above 30 °C and OFF
at or below 28 °C. Inside that interval, the prior valid command persists. This prevents needless rapid switching
when temperature fluctuates near a single trip point.

Now suppose the sensor disappears. Is “last command forever” acceptable? Usually not without a designed and

A supervisory system is more than a thermostat

over UART, and uses LEDs to expose faults. A cooling request becomes ON at 30 °C or above and OFF at 28 °C

requests the FAULT state, with the active output disabled. Upon the next valid sample inside the hysteresis band,
this implementation returns to OFF rather than silently restoring the pre-fault ON command.

close a valve, stop a pump, or run an emergency fan upon sensor failure cannot be decided by a generic C function.

false negative action. Students should write a process-specific safe-state rationale before proposing anything
stronger than an LED.

12.3  An uncertainty budget in plain language
For the 1-kHz test, plausible error contributors include the MSP430 DCO frequency, Pico reference crystal, gate

granularity of about one hertz; the actual system may include other timing errors. If the measurement system later
uses an external GPS 1 PPS signal to characterize its oscillator, confirm GNSS lock, missed pulses, event
selection, and the counter implementation. An average of several intervals reduces some random variation but
cannot by itself eliminate constant bias or misidentified timing edges.

A working demonstration is a beginning, not a finished engineering result. The temptation to report only clean
numbers is particularly strong in classrooms, where failures consume time and a beautiful dashboard can look
more authoritative than a handwritten record. The CAP method counters that temptation by preserving raw
observations, recording the actual instrument configuration, and naming limitations that cannot be removed by
formatting. In industrial automation, these habits are close relatives of calibration control, change management,
commissioning records, and maintenance logs.
Model and technical reasoning
The report should identify the actual hardware and software versions, a question, an a priori prediction, a clear
schematic or annotated wiring photo, raw measurements, calculations, uncertainty discussion, faults encountered,
and a falsifiable conclusion. A sample frequency with many decimal places may be repeatable without being
accurate. A scratchpad with a correct CRC may be internally consistent while reporting the temperature of a badly
mounted sensor rather than the process. The report should make those distinctions visible.
Prediction before measurement
Write one claim you expect to be able to defend about the 1 kHz waveform and another claim you cannot defend
without a calibrated reference. Put a specific test against each.

The historical bridge: what survives
fifty years?
GUIDING QUESTION
Continuity of ideas, not identity of hardware
KEY VOCABULARY
Architecture, modularity, real time, instrumentation, deployment
The PDP-11, launched in 1970, established a notably coherent programming model for small computers. Through
an ecosystem of hardware modules, data acquisition equipment, MACRO-11, and real-time systems such as
RSX-11M, it participated in the physical automation of industrial work. In DEC's own 1977 Introduction to
RSX-11M, oil refineries and steel rolling mills appear as examples of monitored and controlled processes. The
historical claim is not that every industrial controller was a PDP-11, but that the PDP-11 was an important,
documented platform for programmable real-time control.
The MSP430G2553 is a very different machine but retains features worth comparing: a 16-bit programmer's view,
addressable PC and SP, two-operand operations, memory-mapped peripherals, interrupts, and modest enough
resources that machine behavior remains visible. The extra registers and compact constant generator come with
addressing-mode restrictions. Jonathan Kirwan's July 18, 2003 essay, A Perspective on the MSP-430 Instructions
and Addressing Modes, is a useful contemporary discussion of that precise tradeoff. It also reminds us that TI's
RISC marketing label should not replace a careful understanding of instruction encoding and actual execution cost.
The Pico instrument adds a third and equally important idea. Rather than judging the MSP430 by whether a
program compiles, the class measures pulses, examines timing, records error, and publishes evidence. The
development hardware may be sophisticated while the deployed controller is inexpensive. That separation of
development capability from deployment cost is as educationally important as the individual instruction sets.
The historical bridge can be summarized as architecture → real-time operation → physical process →
independent measurement → explainable evidence. This is the course's durable idea. It can be extended to an
STM32, an ESP32, a modern industrial PLC, or a simulated historical minicomputer without losing the
fundamental engineering questions.

validation/HOST_TEST_RESULTS.txt. These tests require no MSP430 and do not involve sensor acquisition.
